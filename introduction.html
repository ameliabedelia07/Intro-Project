Introduction & System Analysis

Problem definition, data provenance, and power structures

Problem Overview

Applying to college is a process that many high school graduates undergo. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 61.4% of recent high school graduates chose to attend college (either two year institutions or four year institutions) in 2023. For many students, finances are a crucial factor, if not the primary determining one, on where to attend college. University gross cost, quality of financial aid, and future job outcomes are all major considerations. Many people believe that there is a direct relationship between cost, whether it’s gross (the “sticker price”) or net (the cost after financial aid), level of selectivity, quality of education, and financial success following graduation. This is largely due to the elitist beliefs surrounding higher education that have not been fully dispelled; highly selective universities often have better connections as they are seen to have more prestige, but they also cost more than their less selective counterparts. Also, in regards to getting admitted into these highly selective institutions, attending a private or boarding school for high school (and earlier) is looked upon very favorably, and these schools are also very pricey. Additionally, families who can afford it may hire companies to help with college applications, getting financial aid documents done accurately and to most benefit the student and their family, which is very beneficial but not necessarily attainable to those with limited income. I digress, however. The perceived benefits of elite colleges encourages students, especially those from limited income households, to apply to colleges more expensive than they can feasibly afford, hoping the tens or potentially hundreds of thousands of student loans they and their families take out will be worthwhile in the long run. Many colleges do have great financial aid departments and limited income domestic students can receive governmental Pell Grants of up to $7,395, but this does not entirely quell the issue. With the rise in popularity of Early Decision (ED) applications, students will bind themselves to a college long before they know what their financial aid would be. This is heavily incentivized by the inflated admittance rates during ED – an extreme case of this is Tulane University which, for its class of 2028, accepted 68% of its ED applicants, 17% of its Early Action (a non binding early application), and only 3% of its Regular Decision (RD) applicants according to Collegiate Gateway. This can seem puzzling, as the same school with the same academic opportunities can, for different applicant pools, either have a very average admittance rate, or one that is on par with the most selective universities in the nation. Why do colleges like Tulane do this? There are two answers: prestige and money. High yield rates (the ratio of applicants who committed to all admitted applicants) and low RD acceptance rates make the university appear desirable by numbers alone, and the binding nature of ED negates any competition from other colleges the student might consider, meaning they can give or withhold however much financial aid they want, within reason. Another thing that can seem puzzling is CNBC’s list of 10 “best” colleges and universities, which emphasizes return on investment (ROI). It includes several Ivies (Penn, Columbia, Harvard, Stanford, Yale), a few Ivy adjacent schools (MIT, Stanford), a top tier liberal arts school (Claremont McKenna), a decently selective but not well known business school (Babson), and… Missouri University of Science and Technology? That is not what one would expect, following the paradigm of selective & expensive → smarter students → better academics → better ROI. Perhaps it doesn’t hold up in nearly as many instances as one would think.
